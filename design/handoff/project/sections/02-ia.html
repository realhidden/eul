Information architecture

Five surfaces, each with one job. The menu bar carries presence and the glance; the panel carries investigation; settings carry rare decisions; widgets carry ambient, stale-tolerant context; notifications carry exactly one message, ever.

2.2 The notch: four approaches, one winner

A — One adaptive wide item (the status quo, evolved). The app guesses what fits and renders one item. Loses: macOS hides a too-wide item whole, and the system never tells you the available width — "adaptive" degrades to guessing, and one wrong guess is total disappearance. The unit of failure remains the entire product. This is the 1.x architecture with better manners; it cannot be made safe.

B — One item per monitor. macOS hides overflowing items individually, so degradation comes free. Loses: relative order is not guaranteed (users ⌘-drag, other apps interleave), so the set reads as scattered fragments rather than one product; the system decides which monitor survives, which may be the least important one; and N items means N status windows for an outcome we still don't control. Maximum architectural cost for delegated, incoherent degradation.

C — Priority collapse inside one item. The app drops monitors by priority as space tightens. Wins on coherence and gives the user a meaningful knob (priority order). But as a complete answer it inherits A's fatal flaw: the final collapsed item can still be hidden whole, and detecting "space is tight" is the hard, unreliable part. Essential as a mechanism; insufficient as an architecture.

D — Anchor + strip (committed). Exactly two status items. The anchor: a fixed-width glyph carrying identity, health, and the click target — it never grows, so it is the cheapest item in the bar to keep. The strip: one adaptive item holding the pinned slots, collapsing by C's priority mechanism down to removal. The sacrifice order is therefore designed, not delegated: slots drop first, the strip vanishes second, the anchor last — and "anchor only" is still a working product (health + entry point + panel). Cost: a two-item architecture and an ordering guarantee that the anchor sits on the system side of the strip so it is hidden last new infra uncertain feasibility. That cost buys the only model where every degradation step is a decision we made.

Verdict: D, with C as its internal mechanism. B is rejected for cost-without-control; A is rejected as the documented failure mode. No hedging — everything below is built on anchor + strip.

2.3 The bar, hi-fi: every state, both modes

Anatomy: slots are fixed-width by their monitor's worst case (CPU 100% reserves the width of 100%), values are tabular — a value changing digit count never moves its neighbors. Labels are 9 pt uppercase secondary; values 12 pt medium. The strip never animates; it re-renders at the refresh cadence.

2.4 Health semantics — what "look at me" means

The glyph has three states. Normal: monochrome template, both eyes outline. Elevated (amber, right eye fills): something deserves a look when convenient. Critical (red, both eyes fill): act soon. Every trigger requires a sustained condition — single samples never change the glyph — and clears only after the signal stays below threshold for twice its trip window (hysteresis), so the glyph cannot flap. All thresholds ship as defaults with per-signal on/off toggles; numeric tuning is deliberately not exposed in v2.0 (P5).

2.5 The recovery moment

When macOS hides even the anchor (the bar truly is full), eul must remain reachable. Three layers, in order of preference:

Detect.

eul observes its status windows' frames; an anchor positioned under the notch or pushed off the item area is "occluded" new infra uncertain feasibility.

Tell, once, silently.

A single passive notification: "eul is hidden — your menu bar is full. Open eul to choose what to show." Posted at most once per occlusion episode, never focus-stealing. This is the only notification in the product.

Always-open escape hatch.

Launching eul while it is already running opens the panel as a centered window with a one-line banner and a button into Menu Bar settings new infra. A global hotkey (default ⌃⌥E) opens the panel regardless of bar state. The 1.x modal alert is deleted.

2.6 The panel — what replaces the dropdown

A Control-Center-style panel anchored to the anchor: opens on click or hotkey, dismisses on click-away or Esc, system material throughout. It answers the glance's follow-up question in one click, and "why" in at most two. While closed it costs nothing; opening it lights up the expensive collectors (per-process CPU/memory/network), exactly as the 1.x menu did — the panel keeps that contract. It is deliberately not a window: no chrome, no tabs, no persistence. Hierarchy replaces the 1.x uniform stack: an abnormal tile gains the only color on the surface, auto-expands, and names its culprit.

Use the scenario button to trip the health engine, then read the panel top-down: verdict → colored tile → culprit. Click the CPU tile for per-core E/P detail; click the FANS tile and “Control fans…” to walk the helper ceremony (§4.6) — set Manual or Boost and the FAN slot pins itself into the bar rig above. Note the footer: eul reports its own footprint on every open — energy honesty as a feature. Fans and Bluetooth tiles exist only where their hardware and build do: on a MacBook Air or in the App Store build the grid simply has fewer tiles, never empty ones.

2.7 Fan control — where the verb lives

Readings and the verb live together. Fans are a panel tile (direct build, fan-equipped Macs only — on a MacBook Air the tile does not exist, rather than sitting empty). Collapsed, it answers the glance: per-fan RPM and the current mode. Expanded, it becomes the control surface (§4.6): per-fan Auto / Manual / Boost, with Manual exposing a slider clamped to the hardware's real min/max and reporting target and actual RPM. Boost is a one-click action inside the expanded tile — deliberately not a bar-level quick action: intervention happens next to the temperatures that justify it, and still lands within the two-interaction budget (anchor → tile).

An active override must be impossible to forget. While any fan is not in Auto: a FAN slot auto-pins to the strip showing live RPM — it is exempt from priority collapse for the duration and disappears the moment control returns to Auto; the panel header carries a second line (“Manual fan override · 23 min”); and “Revert to Auto” is one click in both places. The glyph stays health-only — an override is a choice, not a problem, so it never colors the eyes. State F in Exhibit A shows the bar during an override.

2.8 Out-of-box configuration (14″ MBP)

Default: anchor + CPU slot + network slot — ≈118 pt total. Defense: the two questions Mac users actually ask the bar are "is something eating my machine?" (CPU) and "is my network doing something?" (NET). Memory and thermals are folded into the glyph's health state, so they earn bar pixels only when abnormal — which is precisely when the user wants them. Battery is already in the system bar; duplicating it by default fails P5. Everything else is one click away. Priority order ships as CPU > NET, so under pressure NET drops first.
